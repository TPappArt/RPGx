# RPGx World Overview

The Returning Kingdom

Version 0.2 | 13 October 2026 | Project owner Tibor Papp

This living document defines RPGx and gives game builders a shared reference. A colourful, mysterious frontier stands above a sleeping kingdom. Its restoration threatens the new world, while the ancient catastrophe remains capable of destroying both. Player actions determine destruction, replacement, or lasting coexistence.

## World foundation

### Confirmed premise

The Returning Kingdom is the selected story direction. The older world is a buried civilisation and its former landscape. The precise physical and magical nature of that buried world is still to be defined.

Facing an ancient catastrophe, a powerful civilisation lowered its cities beneath the earth and placed their inhabitants into magical sleep. It preserved its people without permanently defeating the cause of that catastrophe. Surviving descendants established new societies on the surface. Over generations, reliable history faded into records, traditions, and folklore.

The preservation spell was built to restore the old landscape when the sleepers returned. Its designers did not provide a safe way to accommodate the civilisation that now occupies that land. Recreating an ancient river, city, or forest can destroy the settlement, road, or farmland standing in its place.

### Why the crisis begins now

Frontier excavators open a buried sanctuary and awaken its caretaker. Believing the original danger has passed, the caretaker begins the restoration. Each awakened city helps wake the next. The network extends beyond the playable regions. The original danger has not been permanently removed; how its renewed activity connects to the restoration remains open.

The caretaker's identity, knowledge, motives beyond this duty, and possible role as an antagonist remain open. The nature of the original catastrophe and why the sanctuary remained sealed until now also need development.

### What is at stake

Two threats determine the endings. Restoration can erase the present civilisation and return the world to its ancient state. The original catastrophe can destroy the world itself. Stopping immediate destruction does not by itself save the new world, and restoring the old kingdom does not permanently remove the catastrophe. The good ending must resolve both threats.

The sleepers are living people with families, histories, and claims to their homes. Surface communities have equally real attachments and needs. Leaders among the returning people disagree about whether and how the surface inhabitants should be accommodated. The conflict therefore supports negotiation, divided loyalties, and opposition within both societies.

### Tone and creative references

The established approach is colourful, mysterious adventure with an epic story and an increasingly alarming journey. Zelda and World of Warcraft are game references; Farseer, Mistborn, and Stormlight are literary references. Tibor's Arthas comparison guides the protagonist's possible fall into the World Destroyer role. The identity of the evil and its relationship to the caretaker remain open. These references do not establish borrowed lore or specific gameplay systems.

## World structure and discovery

### Map scope

Each inhabited regional map combines open-world exploration, three settlements, and a few dungeons. The current total is tentative. Names, cultures, travel times, dimensions, and exact dungeon counts are open.

| Map type | Current scope | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inhabited regions | Three regions, three settlements each; nine settlements if retained | Tentative total |
| Connecting maps | Two larger maps without towns | Tentative total |
| Dungeons | A few in each inhabited region | Count open |

### Working sequence of discovery

The following sequence organises the approved premise for development. Its exact events and placement across maps are working proposals.

- Wonder: the player encounters a lively frontier and ordinary local needs. Early signs of restoration can appear useful or beautiful.

- Unease: separate incidents suggest something is wrong. Evidence begins contradicting local explanations and recorded history.

- Recognition: the player learns that the changes belong to an older landscape and that its inhabitants are waking.

- Escalation: restoration threatens familiar communities, while the original catastrophe emerges as a second danger. Conflicting interests complicate responses to both.

- Consequence: accumulated actions determine the protagonist's allegiance and whether the world is destroyed, the old kingdom replaces the new, or both survive together.

### Possible signs and story hooks

These examples illustrate the selected concept. They are candidates for later quest design rather than fixed events.

- A dry well refills and unfamiliar flowers spread through farmland. The apparent blessing marks the return of an ancient watercourse.

- An old road emerges beneath the grass and leads towards a buried entrance. Its route crosses places the current map treats as impassable.

- A restored river threatens a village that did not exist when the spell was created.

- A family pendant is recognised by someone in a newly opened ruin, creating a personal connection across the centuries.

Each developed hook should specify the immediate local problem, evidence the player can find, decisions available, and later consequences. The precise operation of these events must follow the magic rules once agreed.

## Endings and future development

### Confirmed ending structure

Three endings depend on player actions. Bad is the almost unavoidable main-story trajectory unless the player seriously works towards another outcome. Neutral and good both require sustained effort. The exact branching conditions, thresholds, and point of commitment remain undecided.

### Confirmed ending outcomes

These outcomes replace the earlier provisional endings. Their underlying events are canon; the quests, costs, mechanisms, and final scenes still need development.

| Ending | Confirmed outcome |
| --- | --- |
| Bad | The player joins the evil and becomes the World Destroyer. The original catastrophe comes to pass and the world is destroyed. This is the most prevalent path. |
| Neutral | The player refuses to join the destruction. World destruction fails for now, but the old kingdom destroys and replaces the new world. The ancient order returns; the catastrophe remains a future possibility. |
| Good | Old and new worlds merge, preserving both. The world-destroying threat is completely and permanently destroyed. Neither civilisation must be erased for the other to survive. |

Proposed design principle: give players understandable evidence and meaningful opportunities to change course. The harder endings should emerge from sustained decisions and preparation. Their precise mechanisms still need agreement.

### Questions to resolve next

- What is the original catastrophe, what evil drives it, and why does it need a World Destroyer?

- How do restoration and catastrophe interact, and what makes merging both worlds possible?

- Who is the caretaker, and how do the returning rulers relate to the evil and the surface inhabitants?

- What draws the protagonist towards the destroyer role, and what enables refusal?

- What cultures, settlements, creatures, and factions inhabit each region?

- Which actions enable each ending, and how is the catastrophe permanently destroyed?
